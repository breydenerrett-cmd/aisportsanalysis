Why we stopped publishing MLB picks

On October 16, 2026 we stopped putting out new MLB picks. Here's why, straight up, including the parts that make us look bad.

What we put out

Since September 22 we put out 178 graded bets: mostly player props, some moneylines. Every one is on the record page, and the losses are the same size as the wins.

How it went

The 178 bets went 97-81 and finished 1.4 units down. That's roughly break-even minus the books' cut, and it's still a loss. The record page's headline numbers are narrower and uglier, and we show them the way the page does, as of October 15: regular-season main picks went 16-17, down 5.05 units, and postseason picks went 27-33, down 9.22 units, graded but not counted in the headline. The 97-81 counts every graded entry; the record page reports 17 withdrawn picks separately. Check the record page for today's numbers.

What we found when we took it apart

Three separate reviews of the same data, then a fourth that reran every number and tried to knock the findings down. Three things survived.

  1. We paid extra on every bet, by design. Our rules only let a bet through when the price was worse than the market's own fair price. That cost about 2.4 points of probability per bet.
  2. Our forecasts had nothing the market didn't. Across 6,818 prop prices, every one and not just the ones we picked, our numbers were slightly worse than the market's. The best weight to give our opinion was zero, or a hair below.
  3. Our ranking picked our worst mistakes. It ranked bets by how far our number sat from the books'. The biggest gaps were where we were most wrong. On the props we put out, our average probability was 61%; they hit 52%; the market had said 52.5%.

The bug

Overs on total bases skipped a correction that overs on hits got. It made those bets look between 2 and 3.5 points better than they were. Fixing it wouldn't have been enough: with all three of our model errors fixed, our forecasts would still sit behind the market's.

Where we got lucky

At the market's own numbers, bets like ours were expected to lose about 7.6 units. We lost 1.4. That's a good run, not skill. We also graded each bet at the best price any book showed. On the part we could check, the board average makes the result about 3.4 units worse.

What happens now

Picks are off. The daily brief, the price board and the graded record keep going, and every pick we made stays on the record page. Picks come back only if a test we set up in advance shows that we beat the closing price. A good run of results won't do it, because luck passes that. Right now the number points the wrong way: on the 82 bets where we could measure it, the price we took was on average 1.7 points worse than the closing price.

What we're not saying

  • That the market can't be beaten, or that nobody can beat it.
  • That 178 bets settle anything. It's a small sample, which is why we looked at prices and forecasts instead of the win-loss total.
  • That the briefs make money. They're analysis, not advice, and unproven.
  • That any of this tells you what comes next.
